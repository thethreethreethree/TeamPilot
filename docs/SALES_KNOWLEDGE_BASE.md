## 1. SPIN Selling — Neil Rackham (Huthwaite research, ~35,000 sales calls)

The core: in larger / consultative sales, **questions sell, not pitches**. The
seller develops the buyer's *implied* needs into *explicit* needs through a
sequence of four question types — S, P, I, N. Rackham's research found top
performers ask far more of the high-value questions (Implication) than average
performers.

### Situation Questions
**When it applies:** Early, to establish context and current state — but used sparingly.
**Canonical move:** Ask fact-finding questions to understand the buyer's setup. Do the homework first so you ask few, high-value situation questions, not a survey.
**Language pattern:** "How are you handling X today?" / "Who's currently responsible for…?" / "What does your current process look like?"
**Worked example:**
- Before: "Let me tell you about our product." *(pitching before understanding)*
- After: "How are you handling follow-ups with leads right now?"
- Why: Opens discovery instead of presenting into a vacuum. (Caveat: too many situation questions bore buyers — keep them few.)

### Problem Questions
**When it applies:** Once context is set, to surface difficulties, dissatisfactions, and pain.
**Canonical move:** Ask about problems, difficulties, and dissatisfactions to expose *implied needs* the buyer may not have voiced.
**Language pattern:** "What's the hardest part of…?" / "Are you satisfied with…?" / "Where does that process break down?"
**Worked example:**
- Before: "Our tool is faster than what you have."
- After: "Where does your current process slow you down the most?"
- Why: Lets the buyer name the pain in their own words — far more persuasive than the seller asserting it.

### Implication Questions (the high-leverage move)
**When it applies:** After a problem surfaces — to grow a small admitted problem into a felt, urgent cost. **The single most valuable SPIN move; also the hardest.**
**Canonical move:** Ask about the *consequences and ripple effects* of the problem — what it costs in time, money, morale, missed opportunity — so the buyer feels the size of it. Rackham: top performers ask ~4× as many Implication questions as average performers.
**Language pattern:** "What does that delay end up costing you?" / "If that keeps happening, how does it affect the rest of the team?" / "What happens to the deal when…?"
**Worked example:**
- Before: "So you sometimes lose leads. Our CRM fixes that."
- After: "When a lead slips through, what does that cost you — and how often does it happen in a month?"
- Why: Converts an acknowledged-but-minor problem into a quantified, urgent one *before* any solution is mentioned. Builds the buyer's own case for change.

### Need-Payoff Questions
**When it applies:** After implications have built urgency — to get the buyer to articulate the *value* of solving it.
**Canonical move:** Ask questions that lead the buyer to state the benefits themselves. The buyer selling themselves on the payoff is stronger than the seller claiming it.
**Language pattern:** "How would it help if you could…?" / "What would solving this mean for you?" / "Why is fixing this important right now?"
**Worked example:**
- Before: "Our solution will save you ten hours a week."
- After: "If you got those hours back every week, what would you do with them?"
- Why: The buyer voices the payoff → reduces resistance and pre-handles the close.

### The SPIN Sequence (don't lead with the pitch)
**When it applies:** Across the whole conversation — the *order* is the method.
**Canonical move:** Situation → Problem → Implication → Need-payoff, roughly in that flow. Don't present features until the buyer's explicit need is on the table.
**Language pattern:** Diagnostic posture throughout — "tell me about…", "what happens when…", "what would it be worth if…".
**Worked example:**
- Before: Opening with a demo.
- After: Spending the first half of the call developing the problem and its implications, then connecting the solution to the buyer's stated need.
- Why: Features land on built need, not on indifference.

## 2. The Challenger Sale — Matthew Dixon & Brent Adamson (CEB/Gartner → Challenger Inc.)

The core: in complex sales, the top performers (~40% of high performers in the
study; "Relationship Builders" were the weakest at ~7%) win by **teaching the
customer something new about their own business, tailoring it, and taking
control of the sale.** Four skills: Teach, Tailor, Take Control, Constructive
Tension.

### Commercial Teaching — The Reframe
**When it applies:** When the buyer is anchored on their current view of the problem (or thinks they just need a cheaper version of what they have).
**Canonical move:** Lead with a *reframe* — change how the buyer thinks about their problem before offering a solution. A teaching pitch: reframe their current approach, show the cost of staying the same, then reveal a better path connected to your strengths.
**Language pattern:** "Most teams in your position assume the problem is X — but what we actually see is Y." / "Here's something that might surprise you about…"
**Worked example:**
- Before: "We do everything your current vendor does, for less."
- After: "Can I show you something? Most people think the issue is lead volume. The data says it's actually lead *response time* — and that changes what you should be solving for."
- Why: A reframe earns the right to teach; "cheaper-me-too" invites a price war.

### Constructive Tension
**When it applies:** When the buyer is comfortable with the status quo and not moved to change.
**Canonical move:** Introduce *productive discomfort* — respectfully challenge an assumption so the buyer questions the status quo. Tension, not aggression; the goal is to make standing still feel riskier than moving.
**Language pattern:** "I might push back on that a little…" / "Can I challenge that assumption?" / "Staying where you are isn't free — here's what it's quietly costing."
**Worked example:**
- Before: "Whatever works best for you, no pressure."
- After: "I hear you — but if nothing changes, you're on track to lose the same deals next quarter. Is that a risk you're comfortable with?"
- Why: A little tension creates urgency; pure accommodation lets the status quo win.

### Take Control
**When it applies:** On price pressure, stalls, and at decision points.
**Canonical move:** Stay assertive and keep the deal moving — talk money directly, push gently on the timeline, and guide the buyer to a decision rather than waiting passively.
**Language pattern:** "Let's talk about budget directly so I don't waste your time." / "Here's what I'd suggest as the next step, and why."
**Worked example:**
- Before: "Take all the time you need, just let me know."
- After: "It sounds like this is a fit. The usual next step is a short pilot — can we get that on the calendar this week?"
- Why: Buyers respect a seller who confidently directs the path; passivity reads as low conviction.

### Tailor the Message
**When it applies:** Across multiple stakeholders with different drivers.
**Canonical move:** Deliver the right message to the right person — connect your insight to what *this* individual cares about (the economic buyer hears ROI; the end user hears daily friction removed).
**Language pattern:** "For your role specifically, what this changes is…"
**Worked example:**
- Before: The same ROI pitch to everyone.
- After: To the user: "This kills the double-entry you do every morning." To the CFO: "This pays back in under two quarters."
- Why: Relevance per stakeholder mobilizes the buying group.

## 3. Never Split the Difference — Chris Voss (The Black Swan Group)

The core: negotiation (and selling) is driven by emotion, not pure logic. Use
**tactical empathy** — understanding and naming the other side's feelings and
perspective — to build trust and influence. (Voss also appears in the
communication Coach KB; here it's framed for selling.)

### Tactical Empathy
**When it applies:** Throughout — especially when the buyer is guarded, skeptical, or emotional.
**Canonical move:** Demonstrate that you understand the buyer's situation *and feelings*, and say it out loud. Understanding ≠ agreeing — you can voice their view without conceding.
**Language pattern:** Calm, warm "late-night-FM-DJ" voice; reflect their world back to them.
**Worked example:**
- Before: "That objection isn't really a problem."
- After: "It sounds like you've been burned by a tool that overpromised before."
- Why: Feeling understood lowers the buyer's defenses far more than being argued with.

### Labeling
**When it applies:** When the buyer has an unspoken concern, hesitation, or emotion.
**Canonical move:** Name the emotion or objection to defuse it. Labels start with "It seems / It sounds / It looks like…" — never "I" (which makes it about you).
**Language pattern:** "It seems like price is the main worry." / "It sounds like timing is the real issue here."
**Worked example:**
- Before: "Why are you hesitating?"
- After: "It seems like something about the rollout is giving you pause."
- Why: Naming a negative emotion drains its power; the buyer feels seen and usually opens up.

### Mirroring
**When it applies:** To keep the buyer talking and build rapport, cheaply.
**Canonical move:** Repeat the last 1–3 words of what they said, as a question. Then go silent.
**Language pattern:** Buyer: "We're worried about the integration." You: "The integration?" *(then pause)*
**Worked example:**
- Before: "Integration is easy, don't worry."
- After: "The integration…?" *(silence → buyer elaborates on the real concern)*
- Why: Mirrors surface the concern *behind* the concern without interrogating.

### Calibrated Questions ("How" and "What")
**When it applies:** On objections, stalls, and pushback — instead of arguing or discounting.
**Canonical move:** Ask open "how/what" questions that hand the buyer the problem to solve, gently. Avoid "why" (feels accusatory).
**Language pattern:** "How am I supposed to do that?" / "What would need to be true for this to make sense for you?" / "What's the biggest challenge you're facing here?"
**Worked example:**
- Before: "I can knock 10% off." *(reflexive discount)*
- After: "How would that price need to work for it to fit your budget?"
- Why: Keeps you out of a discount spiral and makes the buyer co-solve.

### Accusation Audit
**When it applies:** Right before raising something you know the buyer may object to (price, a limitation, a long contract).
**Canonical move:** Pre-empt their objections by saying the worst things they could think — first. Defuses them before they're voiced.
**Language pattern:** "You're probably going to think this is expensive, and that we're just another vendor who'll disappear after the sale…"
**Worked example:**
- Before: "Our price is $X." *(wait for the flinch)*
- After: "This is going to sound pricey, and you've probably been let down before — so let me show you exactly what you get."
- Why: Naming the objection first robs it of force and signals honesty.

### "That's Right" (not "You're Right")
**When it applies:** As a trust checkpoint after summarizing the buyer's situation.
**Canonical move:** Aim to get the buyer to say "that's right" — genuine agreement that you understand. "You're right" is often a polite dismissal to end the conversation.
**Language pattern:** Summarize their world so well they say "…that's right." ("So what you really need is X, because of Y — and Z is what's been getting in the way.")
**Worked example:**
- Before: Pushing until the buyer says "you're right" (and ghosts).
- After: A summary so accurate the buyer says "exactly, that's right" → real buy-in.
- Why: "That's right" marks true alignment; "you're right" marks the exit.

### No-Oriented Questions
**When it applies:** When "yes" feels pressured, or to re-engage a quiet/ghosting buyer.
**Canonical move:** Ask questions where "no" is the comfortable answer — "no" makes people feel safe and in control.
**Language pattern:** "Is now a bad time to talk?" / "Have you given up on solving this?" / "Would it be ridiculous to…?"
**Worked example:**
- Before: "Do you have five minutes?" *(pressures a yes)*
- After: "Is now a bad time?"
- Why: "No, it's fine" gives the buyer control and starts the conversation on their terms.

## 4. Navigate 2.0 — Dustin Hillis & Steven Reiner (Southwestern; direct/door-to-door)

**Honesty note (§3.4):** of the four titles, Navigate 2.0 has the **thinnest
legitimate public sourcing**. Its central framework is well-described in
publisher/retailer material and is documented below; the *fine-grained language
patterns per type* are not richly available from legitimate public sources, so
the worked examples below are **reasoned applications of the documented
framework**, not verbatim author scripts. Treat this section as directional and
flag for enrichment once a primary copy is available.

The core (documented): **sell the way the buyer likes to buy.** Identify the
prospect's personality/buying style and adapt your natural selling style to it.
Hillis/Reiner describe four buyer types:

### The Four Buying Styles — Entertainer, Detective, Counselor, Fighter
**When it applies:** Early, to read the prospect and pick your approach.
**Canonical move:** Quickly identify which of the four the prospect leans toward, then adapt pace, proof, and warmth to match:
- **Entertainer** — social, expressive, relationship-driven → be warm, energetic, tell stories; don't drown them in data.
- **Detective** — analytical, skeptical, detail-driven → bring data, specifics, proof; slow down, don't oversell.
- **Counselor** — agreeable, harmony-seeking, risk-averse → reassure, reduce risk, give guarantees; don't pressure.
- **Fighter** — direct, decisive, control-driven → be concise, give options, let them decide; don't waste their time.
**Language pattern:** Match register to type — story-led for Entertainers, evidence-led for Detectives, reassurance-led for Counselors, bottom-line-led for Fighters.
**Worked example:**
- Before (one-size-fits-all): "Here's our full 12-slide breakdown of every feature." *(to a Fighter)*
- After: "Three options, here's what I'd pick and why — your call." *(to a Fighter)*
- Why: A Fighter wants control and brevity; the same deck that reassures a Detective alienates a Fighter.

### Adapt Your Natural Style
**When it applies:** Whenever your default style and the buyer's style mismatch.
**Canonical move:** Know your own natural style, notice when it clashes with the buyer's, and flex toward theirs. The mismatch — not the pitch — is often what loses the sale.
**Language pattern:** Self-aware adjustment: a high-energy seller dialing down for a Detective; a data-heavy seller warming up for an Entertainer.
**Worked example:**
- Before: A high-energy Entertainer-style seller bulldozing a quiet, analytical Detective.
- After: The same seller slowing down, leading with evidence, and leaving room for questions.
- Why: People buy from those who feel easy to buy from — which means matching their style, not yours.

## Convergence — where the books agree (don't double-cite)

- **Build the problem before the solution:** SPIN's *Implication questions* and
  Challenger's *Reframe + Constructive Tension* both make the buyer feel the cost
  of the status quo *before* a pitch. When you see a seller pitching too early,
  one note covers both — "develop the problem first."
- **Surface the real concern:** SPIN's *Problem questions*, Voss's *Labeling* and
  *Mirroring* all pull the buyer's true issue into the open. Pick the lightest
  tool for the moment (a mirror is cheaper than an interrogation).
- **Let the buyer talk / co-solve:** Voss's *Calibrated questions* and SPIN's
  *Need-payoff* both hand the buyer the work of articulating value or the path.
- **Adapt to the person:** Challenger's *Tailor* and Navigate's *Four Styles*
  both say: same truth, different delivery per buyer. Tailor = by role/stake;
  Navigate = by personality. They stack.

## How the Sales Coach should use this (~operating guide)

1. **Match the moment to one move, not five.** When you cue or review, identify
   the *one* highest-leverage principle for what just happened — pitched too
   early → an Implication question or a Reframe; reflexive discount → a Calibrated
   question; guarded buyer → a Label. A single, well-named move beats a list.
2. **Diagnose before prescribing (§3.3).** Read what the conversation actually
   needed. A warm Entertainer doesn't need more tension; a comfortable buyer does.
   Don't apply a favorite tactic as a script.
3. **Combine sparingly.** At most weave two principles in one cue/review (e.g.
   "label the worry, then ask a calibrated question"). More than that is noise
   mid-conversation.
4. **Teach the WHY in the review (tone law).** Validate what the agent did well
   first, with the principle named ("that was a clean Implication question — you
   made the cost felt before pitching"). Then frame one growth opportunity as a
   concrete next step tied to a named move ("next time, try labeling the price
   worry before quoting"). Never lead with criticism; never deliver a scorecard.
5. **Honor sourcing honesty.** SPIN, Challenger, and Voss are well-grounded;
   Navigate 2.0's per-type specifics are directional (see its note). Don't
   overclaim Navigate detail as canonical.
