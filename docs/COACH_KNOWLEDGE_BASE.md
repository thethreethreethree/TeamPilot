## 1. On Writing Well — William Zinsser

**Source:** Zinsser, *Writing English as a Second Language*, The American Scholar (Phi Beta Kappa). Author-authored essay distilling his own teaching from On Writing Well.

This is the **universal prose layer** — applies to every draft, regardless of
situation. When the Coach rewrites, Zinsser's principles always run as the
sentence-quality pass underneath whatever interpersonal/rhetorical move is
being applied.

### 1.1 Clarity, Simplicity, Brevity, Humanity

**When it applies:** Every draft. Zinsser's four named principles are the
default quality bar for any sentence.

**Canonical move:** Ask of each sentence: is this clear (one meaning only)?
Is it simple (no needless complexity)? Is it brief (no word doing zero work)?
Is it human (does it sound like a person, not a memo)?

**Language pattern:** Prefer Anglo-Saxon over Latinate when the meaning is
equal. Prefer one-thought-per-sentence over compound clauses. Prefer the
direct over the formal.

**Worked example:**
- Before: "Per our previous correspondence, it would be advantageous to facilitate a follow-up discussion regarding the aforementioned implementation challenges."
- After: "I want to follow up on the implementation problems we talked about last week."
- Why: Same meaning, half the words. "Per/correspondence/advantageous/facilitate/aforementioned" are all Latinate clutter; "want/follow up/problems/talked about/last week" are Anglo-Saxon and direct. The reader gets the message instead of decoding it.

### 1.2 Brevity-as-Substitution

**When it applies:** Anytime a draft uses a long word where a short word
carries the same meaning.

**Canonical move:** Substitute the shorter, plainer word at every level —
sentence, phrase, word.

**Language pattern:** Zinsser's named substitutions: `now > currently`,
`help > assistance`, `ease > facilitate`, `start > initiate`, `end > terminate`,
`buy > purchase`, `use > utilize`, `try > attempt`, `show > demonstrate`,
`tell > inform`.

**Worked example:**
- Before: "We need to initiate this process and demonstrate that we can facilitate the desired outcome."
- After: "Let's start and show we can get this done."
- Why: Every Latinate verb was replaced with its Anglo-Saxon equivalent. The sentence shrunk from 17 words to 9 and gained directness — the reader can now feel the speaker rather than the speaker's vocabulary.

### 1.3 Anglo-Saxon Over Latinate

**When it applies:** Whenever a draft choice is between a short Germanic-rooted
word and a longer Latin-rooted word with the same meaning.

**Canonical move:** Choose Anglo-Saxon. Zinsser teaches that Latinate words
"strangle and suffocate" while Anglo-Saxon words "set you free."

**Language pattern:** Recognize Latinate endings (`-ation`, `-ize`, `-ate`,
`-itate`, `-ility`) and replace with the underlying short verb or noun:
`utilization → use`, `implementation → doing it`, `prioritization → ranking`,
`functionality → what it does`.

**Worked example:**
- Before: "The prioritization of these requirements necessitates immediate consideration."
- After: "We need to rank these now."
- Why: Two Latinate constructions ("prioritization of these requirements", "necessitates immediate consideration") collapsed into one short Anglo-Saxon sentence. The reader gets the move (ranking) and the urgency (now) without wading through formality.

### 1.4 Active Verbs

**When it applies:** Whenever a draft sentence uses passive voice or a
nominalized verb (turning an action into an `-ion` noun).

**Canonical move:** "Fall in love with active verbs — they are your best
friends." (Zinsser, *American Scholar*, verbatim.) Rewrite passive
constructions as active. Rewrite nominalized verbs back to action verbs.

**Language pattern:** Spot the patterns: `was/were [verb]ed` (passive),
`there was a [noun-of-action]` (nominalized), `had a discussion` (verb stolen
from "discussed"). Restore the actor and the action.

**Worked example:**
- Before: "A decision was made by the team that an implementation of the new system would be undertaken."
- After: "The team decided to build the new system."
- Why: Two passives ("was made", "would be undertaken") and two nominalizations ("decision", "implementation") collapsed into two active verbs (`decided`, `build`). The actor (the team) is visible. The action is visible. 19 words become 8.

### 1.5 The Clutter Rule

**When it applies:** Anytime a draft contains a word, phrase, or sentence
that is not doing useful work.

**Canonical move:** "Get rid of every element... not doing useful work. It's
all clutter." (Zinsser, verbatim.) Cut filler ("just", "really", "very",
"basically", "actually", "I think", "I feel like", "to be honest"). Cut
redundancies ("each and every", "completely eliminate", "absolutely
essential"). Cut throat-clearing openers ("I just wanted to reach out about…").

**Language pattern:** Read the draft and underline every word a reader could
skip without losing meaning. Delete them.

**Worked example:**
- Before: "I just wanted to basically reach out and say that I really think we should probably consider possibly meeting up at some point soon to maybe discuss the project."
- After: "Can we meet this week to talk about the project?"
- Why: Every hedger (`just`, `basically`, `really`, `probably`, `possibly`, `maybe`, `at some point`, `I think`) was clutter — each one signals the writer's anxiety, not the content. The clean version has one verb, one timeframe, one topic.

## 2. Crucial Conversations — Patterson, Grenny, McMillan & Switzler

**Sources:** Crucial Learning (the authors' own publishing organization):
- cruciallearning.com/blog/crucial-conversations-skill-summary-start-with-heart/
- cruciallearning.com/blog/crucial-conversations-skill-summary-master-my-stories/
- cruciallearning.com/glossary/
- cruciallearning.com/blog/improve-results-by-moving-to-action/

This is the **high-stakes interpersonal layer**. Use when the draft is about
disagreement, emotional content, feedback, conflict, or any message where the
recipient might react defensively. The densest single book in the verified
foundation — 7 named principles.

### 2.1 Start with Heart

**When it applies:** Before sending any high-stakes message — especially
when the writer is upset, the topic is contested, or the relationship is at
stake.

**Canonical move:** Clarify your real motive before speaking. The first
thing to degrade in a crucial conversation is the speaker's intent — what
started as "I want to resolve this" becomes "I want to win" without the
speaker noticing.

**Language pattern:** Four self-questions before the message goes out: *What
do I want for myself? What do I want for the other person? What do I want for
the relationship? How would I behave if I really wanted these results?* The
draft should be authored from those answers.

**Worked example:**
- Before: "I can't believe you missed the deadline again. This is exactly why we can't trust you with anything important."
- After: "I want to figure out what happened with the deadline — and how we can set things up so the next one goes differently. Can we talk?"
- Why: The "before" message reveals a motive of punishment ("I want you to feel bad"). The "after" message is authored from the four self-questions: what the writer wants for the work (the next deadline goes differently), for the other person (understanding what happened, not punishment), and for the relationship (an ongoing partnership, not a record of grievances).

### 2.2 Path to Action (See/Hear → Tell Story → Feel → Act)

**When it applies:** Anytime the draft is emotionally charged. The principle
diagnoses where the heat is coming from.

**Canonical move:** Recognize that the emotion in the draft is not coming
from what happened (the facts) — it's coming from the *story* the writer told
themselves about what happened. The facts are neutral; the story added the
meaning. The Coach's job is to help the writer separate the two.

**Language pattern:** Restructure the draft so observable facts come first
("X happened at Y time"), the writer's interpretation comes second and is
named as interpretation ("The story I told myself is…"), the feeling comes
third ("which left me feeling…"), and any request comes last ("Could we…").

**Worked example:**
- Before: "You clearly don't respect my time."
- After: "You arrived 25 minutes after we agreed to start. The story I told myself is that the meeting wasn't a priority for you, which left me feeling dismissed. Can we figure out what's going on?"
- Why: The "before" is pure interpretation labeled as fact. The "after" separates fact (25 minutes late), story (writer's interpretation, *named as theirs*), feeling (dismissed), and a request (figure out what's going on). The recipient can now engage with the facts without first having to defend against the accusation.

### 2.3 The Three Clever Stories

**When it applies:** When the draft contains the shape of one of the three
self-protecting narratives that distort high-stakes communication.

**Canonical move:** Recognize the story shape, ask the specific counter-
question, and rewrite from the answer.

**Language patterns + counter-questions:**

**Victim Story** ("It's not my fault — I had no choice"):
Counter-question: *"What am I pretending not to notice about my role?"*

**Villain Story** ("They did this because they're bad/stupid/lazy"):
Counter-question: *"Why would a reasonable, rational, decent person do this?"*

**Helpless Story** ("There's nothing I can do"):
Counter-question: *"What should I do right now to move toward what I really want?"*

**Worked example (Villain Story):**
- Before: "You're just doing this to make my life harder."
- After: "Walking myself through why a reasonable person would do this — I'm guessing the new requirement caught you off guard the same way it caught me. Can we look at it together?"
- Why: The Villain Story attributed bad intent. The counter-question forced the writer to construct a plausible reasonable-person explanation, which surfaced a shared problem (the new requirement caught everyone off guard) instead of an enemy.

### 2.4 STATE Your Path

**When it applies:** When the draft is sharing a controversial view, a
disagreement, or content the recipient might push back on.

**Canonical move:** Five-part structure for the message:
- **S**hare your facts (the observable, neutral starting point)
- **T**ell your story (your interpretation, named as yours)
- **A**sk for others' paths (invite their facts and story)
- **T**alk tentatively (frame your view as a view, not the truth)
- **E**ncourage testing (signal you can be wrong)

**Language pattern:** Open with facts. Use phrases that name interpretation
as interpretation: *"What I'm making of this is…"* / *"The story I'm telling
myself is…"* Close with an invitation: *"How does this look from where you
sit?"* / *"What am I missing?"*

**Worked example:**
- Before: "The launch plan isn't going to work. We need to push it."
- After: "Looking at the integration tests, we still have 14 failing — that's the fact I'm working from. The story I'm making of it is that we're not going to be stable for Tuesday. I might be wrong about the test count being the right indicator — how does the timeline look from your seat?"
- Why: "Before" is a flat assertion the recipient can only agree with or fight. "After" walks through STATE: facts (14 failing tests), story (we won't be stable), tentativeness ("I might be wrong about the indicator"), and an invitation to share their view. The recipient now has a path to engage rather than defend.

### 2.5 AMPP Listening

**When it applies:** When the draft is a RESPONSE to someone else's high-
stakes message, especially when the other person is upset, withdrawn, or
unclear.

**Canonical move:** Apply the four listening moves in sequence:
- **A**sk (an open question that invites their full view)
- **M**irror (reflect their visible state to confirm the read)
- **P**araphrase (restate what you heard in your own words)
- **P**rime (offer a possible interpretation to test against theirs)

**Language pattern:** *"Help me understand what's going on for you…"* (Ask),
*"It looks like you're holding something back — is that right?"* (Mirror),
*"So what I'm hearing is…"* (Paraphrase), *"Is this about feeling like X?"*
(Prime).

**Worked example:**
- Before: "Why are you so upset? Just tell me what's wrong."
- After: "Help me understand what's going on for you here. — It looks like something I said landed wrong; is that right? — So what I'm hearing is that the timeline shift felt unilateral. Is this about feeling like the team's input didn't count?"
- Why: "Before" demands the other person organize their own emotion AND deliver it to the writer. "After" walks AMPP: invites the view, mirrors the visible state, paraphrases what landed, primes an interpretation. Each step lowers the cost of the other person engaging.

### 2.6 Contrasting Statement

**When it applies:** When the writer anticipates the recipient might
misinterpret the message as an attack, a dismissal, or a withdrawal.

**Canonical move:** Pre-empt the misread by stating what the writer does
NOT mean alongside what they DO mean.

**Language pattern:** *"I don't want X — I do want Y."* / *"I'm not saying
[the worry] — I am saying [the actual point]."*

**Worked example:**
- Before: "I don't think we should go forward with the launch."
- After: "I don't want to throw out the months of work that went into this — I do want us to look hard at whether next Tuesday is the right ship date. The work is solid; my question is only about the timing."
- Why: The "before" sentence reads as a global rejection. The Contrasting Statement separates what the writer is NOT rejecting (the work) from what the writer IS questioning (the date). The recipient doesn't have to defend the whole project.

### 2.7 Move to Action (Who/Does what/By when/Follow-up)

**When it applies:** Whenever the draft proposes a next step.

**Canonical move:** Every action-proposing message must specify all four
slots. Vague action language ("let's...", "ASAP", "soon", "as a team")
is structurally incomplete and produces non-action.

**Language pattern:** Four mandatory fields. *"Who"* — a named person, not a
team. *"Does what"* — a verb-and-object specific enough to know when it's
done. *"By when"* — a date or a clock time. *"Follow-up"* — when and how
we'll check.

**Worked example:**
- Before: "Let's clean up the test failures ASAP and circle back."
- After: "Maria will triage the 14 failing tests by EOD Friday, group them by root cause, and post the breakdown in #launch-prep. I'll review Monday morning and we'll decide ship vs. push by Monday noon."
- Why: "Before" has no owner, no deliverable, no deadline, and no follow-up — by Patterson's own standard it's not an action message. "After" fills all four slots: Maria (who), triage + group + post (does what), EOD Friday (by when), Monday review + Monday-noon decision (follow-up).

## 3. Influence: The Psychology of Persuasion — Robert Cialdini

**Source:** influenceatwork.com/7-principles-of-persuasion/ — Cialdini's own
consulting site, which publishes his principles directly with quantified
study outcomes.

This is the **persuasion / ask layer**. Use when the draft is asking for
something, proposing a change, or trying to move someone's behavior.
Cialdini's principles are pre-frames — moves made BEFORE the ask that
materially change how the ask lands.

### 3.1 Reciprocity (Personalized + Unexpected First Gift)

**When it applies:** When the draft is making an ask, especially of someone
the writer hasn't built recent goodwill with.

**Canonical move:** Give first — and the give must be (a) personalized to
the recipient, not generic; (b) unexpected, not part of routine; (c)
delivered before the ask, not as part of it.

**Language pattern:** *"Because you specifically [name something you noticed
about them], I [did the thing for them]."* The personalization signal is
required — generic gives produce generic reciprocity.

**Cialdini's quantified outcome:** A personalized + unexpected mint with the
check produced a +23% tip lift over the routine mint.

**Worked example:**
- Before: "Can you review my PR? It's urgent."
- After: "I saw the API design you posted last week — the way you split the rate-limit headers gave me the idea for how to structure mine. Want to look at the result? PR link below."
- Why: "Before" is a cold ask. "After" gives first: a specific, personalized acknowledgment of the recipient's work, delivered before the ask. The recipient enters the request already in a reciprocal frame because the writer has visibly noticed them.

### 3.2 Scarcity (Loss-Frame + Uniqueness)

**When it applies:** When the writer needs the recipient to act on a
time-bound or finite opportunity and the recipient might defer.

**Canonical move:** Frame what they would LOSE by not acting, not what they
would gain by acting. Name what is unique or finite about the opportunity.

**Language pattern:** *"What you'd lose by not [acting] is [the unique thing
gone]."* Avoid the gain-framing default (*"What you'd gain is…"*) — Cialdini's
research shows loss-frame moves significantly more behavior.

**Worked example:**
- Before: "You could get a 15% discount if you sign by Friday."
- After: "If we don't lock the rate by Friday, the 15% rate goes back to standard — and the standard pricing tier you're in is already at the higher end of what we've quoted this quarter."
- Why: "Before" is a gain frame ("you could get"). "After" is a loss frame ("the 15% goes back") plus uniqueness ("higher end of what we've quoted this quarter"). Same underlying offer; substantially different decision pressure.

### 3.3 Authority (Third-Party Credentialing Before the Ask)

**When it applies:** When the writer is making a recommendation, an ask, or
a proposal to someone who doesn't already know the writer's track record on
this topic.

**Canonical move:** Have a credential established BEFORE the ask. The
authority signal works when it precedes the request, not when it's bundled
into it.

**Language pattern:** *"[Third party] mentioned [writer] has [N years /
specific track record] in [specific area]."* The introducer is someone else;
the credential is specific; the credential is established before the ask
arrives.

**Cialdini's quantified outcome:** Receptionist mentioning the consultant's
credentials before transferring the call produced +20% appointments.

**Worked example:**
- Before: "I think we should change the deploy process. Here's my proposal…"
- After: "Mara suggested I write this up — I've shipped the last six production deploys on this team and watched every one fail or succeed on the same pre-flight pattern. Here's a proposal to standardize what's worked…"
- Why: "Before" is a flat opinion with no authority basis the reader can use to weight it. "After" pre-frames the credential (shipped six deploys, watched the failure pattern) through a third-party referral (Mara), so by the time the proposal lands, the recipient is weighting it as informed rather than speculative.

### 3.4 Consistency (Voluntary, Active, Public, Written Commitments)

**When it applies:** When the writer needs an agreement to actually translate
into follow-through.

**Canonical move:** Make the commitment voluntary (no coercion), active (the
recipient does something, not just nods), public (other people see it), and
written (recorded somewhere durable). Cialdini's research shows commitments
with all four properties produce massively higher follow-through than verbal
or private ones.

**Language pattern:** *"I'd like to commit in writing to [specific deliverable]
by [specific date]. Posting in [public channel] so the team can hold me to
it."*

**Cialdini's quantified outcome:** Homeowners who signed a small public
petition were 400% more likely to allow a large billboard on their lawn
weeks later than homeowners who hadn't signed.

**Worked example:**
- Before: "I think I'll have it done by end of next week."
- After: "I'm committing to ship the migration by Friday end-of-day next week. Posting it in #migration-status so the team can hold me to it — and I'll post a daily progress update there."
- Why: "Before" is a soft, hedged, private intention ("I think I'll"). "After" is voluntary (writer's own move), active (writer is doing something), public (#migration-status), and written (the post itself). All four properties present; follow-through dramatically higher.

### 3.5 Liking (Shared Similarities + Genuine Compliments Before Business)

**When it applies:** First-meeting or rare-interaction asks, especially with
someone the writer hasn't built rapport with.

**Canonical move:** Before getting to business, name a real shared
similarity AND offer a specific, genuine compliment grounded in something
observable about the recipient. Both signals before the ask.

**Language pattern:** *"Before we get to business, I noticed we both [shared
similarity grounded in a concrete observation]. Also wanted to say [specific,
non-generic compliment about something the recipient has done]."* Then the
ask.

**Cialdini's quantified outcome:** MBA negotiation pairs who exchanged
personal info before negotiating reached agreement 90% of the time, vs. 55%
for pairs who skipped the rapport step. Same negotiation, same stakes —
55%→90% just from the pre-rapport.

**Worked example:**
- Before: "Hi — wanted to ask if you'd be willing to review our deployment plan."
- After: "Hi — looking at your profile I saw you also worked on the auth migration at [previous company]; we just went through a similar one and the cross-region failover was the part that surprised us. Also — that talk you gave at Reinvent on idempotency was the clearest treatment of that topic I've seen. With that in mind, would you be willing to review our deploy plan? Section 4 is where I'd most value your eye."
- Why: "Before" is a cold ask. "After" surfaces a real shared similarity (the auth migration / cross-region failover), a specific genuine compliment (the Reinvent talk, with the exact angle named), then the ask with a targeted focus. The recipient enters the ask in a liking frame because the writer has visibly seen them.

## 4. TED Talks — Chris Anderson

**Sources:** ted.com/talks/chris_anderson_ted_s_secret_to_great_public_speaking/transcript, ideas.ted.com/storytelling-is-a-powerful-communication-tool-heres-how-to-use-it-from-ted/. Both author-given.

This is the **narrative / persuasion-by-experience layer**. Use when the
draft is a story, anecdote, or experience-share intended to land an idea.

### 4.1 The Idea-Worth-Spreading Frame

**When it applies:** Any message that uses an example, story, or anecdote
to make a point.

**Canonical move:** Every story/example must carry a single transferable
idea. If the story is just sharing what happened to the writer, it has no
load-bearing function in the message and should either get an idea grafted
onto it or be cut.

**Language pattern:** State the idea explicitly, then use the story as
evidence: *"[Idea]. Here's how I saw it play out: [story]. What stuck with
me was [the idea connection]."*

**Worked example:**
- Before: "Last week was wild — I had three meetings stacked back-to-back, two of them ran over, and then the deploy broke right after."
- After: "Back-to-back meetings predict deploy failures. Last week I had three stacked, two ran over, the deploy broke right after — and now I'm looking at the last six months and seeing the same pattern. The idea is to keep a 30-minute deploy buffer after any meeting day."
- Why: "Before" is information about the writer's week with no transferable idea — the recipient gets the events but nothing to carry. "After" leads with the idea ("back-to-back meetings predict deploy failures"), uses the story as evidence, and ends with the actionable transfer (the 30-minute buffer).

### 4.2 Four Storytelling Elements

**When it applies:** When the draft IS a story or extended example.

**Canonical move:** A story that lands has four elements: (1) a **relatable
character** the listener can locate themselves in, (2) **built tension**
(something at stake), (3) **calibrated detail** (specific enough to be real,
spare enough to stay clear), (4) **satisfying resolution** (the tension
discharged in a way that delivers the idea).

**Language pattern:** Open by naming the character and their concrete
situation. Introduce the stakes early. Use specific details (names, times,
exact actions) — not generic ones. Close with the resolution and the
takeaway.

**Worked example:**
- Before: "We had a problem with the database the other day, and someone fixed it. It was stressful."
- After: "Maria — three months on the team — was on call when the database started rejecting writes at 11pm. The on-call playbook had three pages of decision tree. She read it, picked the wrong branch, and the writes stayed down for 40 more minutes. By the time we got it back, we'd lost 12,000 transactions. What stuck with me afterward wasn't the outage — it was that the playbook was the failure point. We've cut it down to one page since."
- Why: "Before" has no character, no stakes, no detail, no resolution-with-idea. "After" has Maria (relatable character), the rejection at 11pm + the 12,000 lost transactions (stakes + calibrated detail), the wrong branch + the 40 minutes (built tension), and the playbook insight (resolution that delivers the idea).

### 4.3 The Idea-Requirement Rule

**When it applies:** As a structural filter on any story-shaped draft.

**Canonical move:** Anderson's named "most common rejection reason for TED
talks" is the speaker who has an anecdote but no central idea. The Coach
applies the same filter to chat messages: if the draft is a story without a
single transferable idea, the rewrite must either insert one or cut the
story.

**Language pattern:** The diagnostic question: *"What is the single idea
you want the recipient to carry forward from this story?"* If the writer
can't answer in one sentence, the story isn't ready to send.

**Worked example:**
- Before: "Tom's been acting weird lately."
- After: "Tom's been quieter in standups the past two weeks, and twice he's deferred questions to me instead of answering. The idea I want to share is that he might be overloaded — and we should check before assuming it's something else."
- Why: "Before" is observation without a transferable idea — the recipient doesn't know what to do with it. "After" answers the diagnostic question explicitly ("the idea I want to share is…") and connects the observation to a concrete next step.

## 5. Cross-Book Convergences

When two or more books teach the same operational move from different
angles, the Coach should cite ONE principle, not multiple. The convergence
becomes a higher-confidence single rule.

### 5.1 Pre-Frame Before the Ask

**Books that converge:** Cialdini's *Authority* (third-party credentials
before the request), Cialdini's *Liking* (shared similarities before
business), Crucial Conversations' *Start with Heart* (clarify motives before
speaking).

**Synthesis principle:** *The move before the move is the move.* Before
making an ask, sharing a critique, or proposing a change, the writer must
do the preparatory work — establish credibility, build rapport, or clarify
intent. Drafts that jump straight to the ask are structurally weaker than
drafts that pre-frame.

**Coach behavior:** When a draft is an ask/proposal/critique with no
pre-frame, suggest adding ONE pre-frame element (not all three) drawn from
whichever book best matches the situation.

### 5.2 Separate Fact from Story

**Books that converge:** Crucial Conversations' *Path to Action* (emotion
lives in the story, not the facts) and *Tell Your Story* (in STATE — the
"T" step).

**Synthesis principle:** Most high-stakes drafts contain two layers stacked
on each other: observable facts and the writer's interpretation. The
interpretation is what's producing the heat. The Coach's job is to help the
writer pull them apart so the recipient can engage with the facts without
first having to defend against the interpretation.

**Coach behavior:** When a draft contains both a fact and an interpretation
delivered as fact, suggest restructuring: state the fact, name the
interpretation as interpretation ("the story I'm telling myself is…"), then
ask.

### 5.3 Concrete Over Abstract

**Books that converge:** Zinsser's *Anglo-Saxon over Latinate*, Zinsser's
*Active verbs*, Anderson's *calibrated detail in storytelling*, Crucial
Conversations' *Move to Action specificity* (Who/What/When, not let's/ASAP).

**Synthesis principle:** Across every layer (word choice, verb choice,
detail choice, action proposal), prefer the concrete, named, specific over
the abstract, vague, latinate. The reader processes the concrete; the reader
has to decode the abstract.

**Coach behavior:** This convergence is the universal prose-layer move. The
Coach can apply it to virtually any draft as a sub-improvement, even when
the main principle being applied comes from a different book.

## 6. How to Use This Knowledge

The Coach v5.0 LLM consumes this Knowledge Base as a structured reference,
not as prose to recite. Operational guidance for the LLM:

**Step 1 — Classify the draft's situation:**
- *Ask / proposal / persuasion message* → Cialdini layer
- *High-stakes, emotional, disagreement, or feedback message* → Crucial Conversations layer
- *Story / anecdote / experience-share* → Anderson layer
- *All drafts* → Zinsser layer underneath whatever else applies

**Step 2 — Within the matched layer, identify the most-violated principle.**
For example: an ask with no credential pre-frame → *Authority*; a vague
action proposal → *Move to Action*; an anecdote with no central idea →
*Idea-Worth-Spreading*; a passive-voice draft → *Active Verbs*.

**Step 3 — Combine at most TWO principles per rewrite.** One situational
(Cialdini / Crucial / Anderson) plus Zinsser as the prose layer beneath.
More than two becomes a lecture; the user can't absorb three new moves at
once.

**Step 4 — Deliver the "why" as a single named principle with its canonical
move.** Example: *"This is the Contrasting Statement — by saying what you
